# study-of-wombat-poop

A lighthearted but scientifically rigorous repository documenting a field-study of wombat scat (poop, droppings, feces) for educational, ecological, and biological research purposes.

---

## Overview

This repository collects protocols, notes, and imagery related to the identification, measurement, and interpretation of wombat feces. Our aim is to provide an organized, reproducible baseline for field biologists, students, and citizen scientists who want to learn from non-invasive signs of wombat presence and health.

What you'll find here:
- Practical morphology notes and measurement best-practices
- Habitat and diet inference guidance from scat characteristics
- Ethics, safety, and permitting considerations
- An image gallery of representative samples
- Suggestions for future analyses and collaboration

Tone: professional, evidence-focused — with a little natural-history delight. Wombats produce uniquely shaped scat and that has a lot to tell us.

---

## Why it matters

Scat is one of the most accessible, non-invasive sources of data about a free-ranging mammal. For wombats specifically:
- Distinctive cube-shaped feces can help confirm species presence and identify latrine sites.
- Scat analysis reveals diet composition, seasonal changes, parasite loads, and gut microbiome signals.
- Spatial patterns of droppings can inform territoriality, population density estimates, and conservation planning.
- Community-driven scat collection can engage local groups in monitoring and habitat restoration.

Reliable scat records help conservationists make better-informed decisions with minimal disturbance to animals.

## Morphology notes

Wombat scat has several diagnostic traits. Record these systematically:

Key descriptors
- Shape: famously cuboidal in many species — note whether pieces appear rectangular, rounded, or irregular.
- Size: measure length, width, and height (mm). Use calipers for consistency.
- Mass: record wet and (if appropriate) dry mass (g).
- Color: record as fresh/aged and color (greenish, brown, dark brown, gray), noting diet seasonality.
- Consistency: classify (firm/dry, firm/moist, crumbly, loose).
- Surface texture: smooth, ridged, crumbly.
- Numbering/packaging: single pieces vs. stacked clusters typical of latrines.

Suggested metadata fields for each sample
- sample_id (unique)
- date, time
- GPS coordinates (WGS84)
- substrate (rock, soil, grass, den entrance)
- species (if observed)
- measurements (length, width, height, mass)
- photographer (credit)
- collector and permit ID (if required)
- notes (odor, insect activity, apparent age)

Measurement tips
- Photograph with scale (ruler or coin) and hand-held color card when possible.
- For fragile/traveling collections, air-dry and store in clean paper envelopes; use sterile containers for microbiome/parasite work.
- Avoid compressing cubes when photographing — use gentle handling.

## Habitat / Diet clues

Scat offers direct clues to what wombats are eating and where they are spending time.

Diet indicators in scat
- Grass blades and grass seed fragments → grazing diet (common in many wombats).
- Bark, roots, fibrous plant material → foraging on tougher vegetation.
- Seed types and undigested plant fragments can sometimes be identified to genus or species.
- High ash content / dark color may indicate increased grazing of burnt regrowth in post-fire landscapes.

Habitat inferences
- Clustered scat (latrine sites) often occurs on prominent rocks, logs, or trail junctions and can indicate territorial boundaries or travel corridors.
- Scat near burrow entrances suggests den occupancy; repeated fresh samples over time suggest active use.
- Scat on elevated rock surfaces might indicate communication/marking behavior rather than feeding location.

Combine scat observations with vegetation surveys and camera-trap data for robust habitat/diet inference.

## Ethics / Safety

Professional and ethical practice is essential.

Permits and permissions
- Obtain local wildlife permitting before collecting samples in protected areas.
- Respect private property — secure landowner permission.

Personal safety and biosecurity
- Treat all fecal material as potentially infectious. Zoonotic pathogens can be present.
- Always use PPE: nitrile gloves, eye protection if necessary, and wash hands thoroughly after handling.
- Use sterile tools/containers for samples destined for lab analysis. Seal and label containers.
- Disinfect tools and surfaces after sample handling (70% ethanol or 10% bleach solutions, following safety guidance).
- Minimize disturbance to animals — do not approach or attempt to trap animals to obtain samples.

Data ethics
- Mask precise GPS locations of sensitive populations in public datasets or follow data-sharing agreements with conservation partners.
- Acknowledge contributors and ensure data provenance is tracked.

## Future work

Planned and suggested directions:
- Standardized protocol document and datasheet template (CSV/JSON) for field teams.
- Photogrammetry and 3D scanning to quantify cube morphology across individuals and populations.
- Machine learning classifier to identify wombat scat versus other species from images.
- Microbiome and parasite profiling of scat samples to assess health and population disease dynamics.
- Citizen-science platform for latrine mapping with data validation and privacy controls.
- Comparative studies to test hypotheses on the biomechanics of cuboid feces formation.

If you want to collaborate or propose a study, please open an issue or contact the repository maintainers.
